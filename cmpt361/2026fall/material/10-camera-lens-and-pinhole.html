The ideal camera

An imaginary camera with two magic powers: its single piece of glass can re-shape itself to any focal length f you ask for, and its box can stretch to put the sensor at any distance d. Nothing like this exists. Play with it anyway — what it has to do to form a sharp image is exactly what a real lens spends all its glass trying to approximate.

What to watch. Every ray leaving a single object point strikes a different part of the glass, and all of them reconvene at one image point. That reconvening is the whole job. Where it happens is set by f and the object's distance, and nothing else — so pick f, then stretch the box until d lands on sᵢ and the sharp dot appears. You will find you can never satisfy A, B and C at once: one d, one sharp depth, everything else a disc.

Now notice what the camera had to do. Dial f down and the glass swells into a fat bulge; dial it up and it flattens almost to a plate. Rack d out and the body distends into a long tube. A real camera can do neither — its glass is cut once and never changes, and its box is a fixed shell. Tab 2 is about the trick real lenses use to fake these two freedoms with a stack of fixed elements that merely slide.

Tab 3. Close the opening down and the two pictures converge. They were never far apart: the ray through the centre of the lens is undeviated, so it is the very ray a pinhole would have passed — which is why the blur disc is always centred on the pinhole prediction, −d·y/s₀, in focus or not. Shrinking the opening simply removes every ray except that one. That is the licence for the rest of the course: to work out where a 3D point lands we can throw the glass away and keep a single hole. The lens was never about the geometry. It was about gathering enough light that the hole does not have to be tiny.

The third band is the last move, and it is pure bookkeeping. A pinhole image is upside down, which is a nuisance to carry through every equation. So we take the same straight line and read it off one plane earlier — a screen placed in front of the hole, at the same distance d. Nothing physical sits there; it is the same ray, same similar triangles, same d. But the image is upright and the minus sign is gone: y′ = d·y/s₀. That is the camera every projection matrix in this course is written for — one point, one plane in front of it, and no glass anywhere.
