One image, four kernels

The same photograph runs through all four steps, so the only thing changing down the page is the 3 × 3 matrix in the middle. Start with the kernel that does nothing, blur it, take the difference of those two, and build a sharpener out of the difference.

Convolution is linear in its kernel, so the subtraction can move inside — from after the convolution to before it. Two filters and a difference collapse into a single filter. Everything below is one instance of this: put the identity in for k1 and a blur in for k2, and the kernel you are left with on the right is a high-pass filter.

4  Put the detail back — sharpen δ + αh

Take the original and add some of its own high-pass back on top. Same move again: I + α(I ⊛ h) = I ⊛ (δ + αh), so it is one kernel and one pass. The weights still sum to one, so nothing gets brighter or darker overall — the edges simply get steeper.

The box case is the familiar one. Identity minus box blur comes out as &frac19; of [−1 −1 −1; −1 8 −1; −1 −1 −1] — the edge kernel students usually meet as something handed down from nowhere. It is not arbitrary. It is doing nothing, with a blur subtracted from it.

Low and high are relative to the blur. There is no absolute dividing line. Switch between the box and the Gaussian: the Gaussian is the gentler blur, so it discards less, so its high-pass picks up less. The same photograph gives a different “high” band depending only on what you subtracted.

The two halves add back up. Since h = δ − g, we have g + h = δ, so the blurred image plus the high-pass image reconstructs the original exactly — no information is lost, only split into two piles. That is the honest sense in which a blur and its complement partition the image.

At the border. Pixels outside the edge are taken to repeat the nearest real pixel. Every step here uses the same rule, which is what makes the two routes in step 3 agree to floating-point dust rather than merely closely.
