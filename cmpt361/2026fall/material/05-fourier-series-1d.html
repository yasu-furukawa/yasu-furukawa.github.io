Why each frequency needs two numbers

A wave at frequency k can sit anywhere in its cycle. One cosine alone can't express that — it always starts at its peak. So each frequency gets a pair: akcos(2πkt) + bksin(2πkt). The cosine carries the part of the curve that's symmetric about t = 0, the sine carries the antisymmetric part, and together they can land the wave at any height and any shift.

That same pair can be written as one shifted cosine, Akcos(2πkt − φk), with Ak = √(ak² + bk²) and φk = atan2(bk, ak). Same information, read as size and shift instead of as two ingredients.

Which is what a complex number is for

Plot the pair (ak, bk) as a point in a plane — the small squares in the right-hand column. Its distance from the origin is the amplitude; its angle is the phase. A point in a plane whose length and angle are the meaningful quantities is exactly a complex number: ck = ak + i bk.

Nothing mystical is happening. The complex number is bookkeeping that keeps the two components fastened together, and it earns its keep because shifting a wave in time becomes plain multiplication by eiθ. (A library DFT hands you the conjugate, ak − i bk; same length, opposite-sign angle.)

One honest caveat: this treats your curve as one period of something that repeats forever. Draw a curve whose ends don't match and you'll see overshoot at the edges — Gibbs ringing, and a genuine property of the series, not a bug in the page.
