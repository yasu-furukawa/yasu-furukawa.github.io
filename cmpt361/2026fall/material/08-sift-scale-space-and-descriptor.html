SIFT: scale space and the descriptor

Harris tells us where corners are, but not how big they are, and it can’t tell two corners apart. SIFT adds both. First it searches every scale for blobs, then it turns the neighbourhood of each keypoint into a 128-number fingerprint that survives rotation and lighting changes.

Finding keypoints across scales

Each octave blurs the image repeatedly, L(σ) = G(σ) ∗ I, with σ growing by k = 21/3 each step. Subtracting neighbours gives the difference of Gaussians D(σ) = L(kσ) − L(σ), a cheap stand-in for the scale-normalised Laplacian. After three steps σ has doubled, so the image is halved and the next octave starts.

Each red-outlined D is the difference of the two outlined L images above it. Faded ends have no neighbour on one side, so only the middle three are searched for extrema.

σ = 1.6

Is this pixel an extremum?

Tap any pixel in the difference image. A candidate must beat all 26 neighbours: 8 in its own scale and 9 in each scale above and below.

    Keeping the good ones

    Candidates are refined to sub-pixel accuracy with a quadratic fit, then two tests throw out weak ones.

    Lowe uses 0.03 and r = 10, on an image doubled in size first. We skip the doubling, so the default here is a little lower to keep a similar number of keypoints.

    Describing one keypoint

    Everything happens in the blurred image L at the keypoint’s own scale, so a blob twice as large is described with a window twice as large. Tap a keypoint to describe it.

    aPick a keypoint

    bDominant orientation

    Gradient directions near the keypoint vote into 36 bins, weighted by magnitude and a Gaussian of 1.5σ. The tallest peak sets the patch’s “up”. Any other peak within 80% of it makes an extra keypoint with its own orientation.

    c16 × 16 gradient samples

    The rotated window is sampled on a 16 × 16 grid. Arrows show each sample’s gradient, already shrunk by a Gaussian weight (σ = half the window) so the edges of the window matter less.

    d4 × 4 cells of 8-bin histograms

    Each 4 × 4 block of samples becomes one histogram with 8 directions. The arrows here are the 8 bin heights. Soft binning spreads each sample across the neighbouring cells and bins, so a small shift doesn’t make values jump.

    eThe 128-dimensional vector

    16 cells × 8 bins = 128 numbers, laid out cell by cell. Normalising to unit length cancels contrast changes. Capping each entry at 0.2 and normalising again limits the influence of a few very strong gradients, such as from lighting or 3-D effects.
