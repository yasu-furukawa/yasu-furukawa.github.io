Sampling, and what it does to the spectrum

Sampling is two operations, not one. Pick — multiply by an impulse train, which copies the spectrum over and over. Paint — convolve with a reconstruction kernel, which tries to keep one copy and throw away the rest. Nyquist is the question of whether those copies overlap.

The step the blackboard leaves out

The objection is correct, and it is not a small one. The impulse-sampled signal f · comb is a row of infinitely sharp spikes, so its spectrum really does run to infinity — copies of everything, including DC and the low frequencies, at ±1/T, ±2/T, and on forever. As a thing to look at, it would be hideous. That is true even when nothing has aliased.

The resolution is that nobody ever looks at it. It is an intermediate object that exists so the algebra can be written down. What reaches your eye is the reconstruction, and the reconstruction filter is not an optional nicety you apply if you feel like it — it is performed by hardware whether or not the diagram admits it. A monitor does not emit impulses. Each pixel is a small glowing rectangle of finite width, and that rectangle is the box kernel. Your eye’s optics and retina lowpass it further.

Which gives the everyday example worth putting on the board: the visible pixel grid of a low-resolution screen is the leftover copy at 1/T. Sit close and you see the grid; step back, or defocus, and it disappears — you have just improved the reconstruction filter. Nothing about the samples changed.

  1. The signal. Bandlimited, finite, well behaved.
  2. Multiply by the comb. Copies every 1/T, out to infinity. Aliasing is decided here and nowhere else. This object is never displayed.
  3. Convolve with the reconstruction kernel. The display aperture, the optics, the eye. The blackboard usually stops before this row.
  4. What you actually see. One copy, plus whatever the kernel failed to suppress.

The distinction students miss

A better interpolator cannot repair aliasing. Step 2 decides whether the information still exists; step 3 only decides how well you display what remains. Switch the kernel to Sinc while the signal is aliased — the reconstruction becomes perfectly smooth and perfectly wrong.

Writing it down

Pick. The impulse train is the one object whose transform is another impulse train, which is exactly why sampling is written this way:

$$ \sum_n \delta(x-nT) \;\longleftrightarrow\; \frac{1}{T}\sum_k \delta\!\left(\nu-\frac{k}{T}\right) $$

so multiplying by it in space convolves by it in frequency, and the spectrum is copied at every multiple of \(1/T\):

$$ f_s(x)=f(x)\sum_n \delta(x-nT) \;\longleftrightarrow\; F_s(\nu)=\frac{1}{T}\sum_k F\!\left(\nu-\frac{k}{T}\right) $$

Nyquist. The copies miss each other exactly when \(F\) vanishes above half the sampling rate:

$$ F(\nu)=0 \ \text{ for } |\nu|\ge \tfrac{1}{2T} \qquad\Longleftrightarrow\qquad \text{no overlap} $$

Paint. Reconstruction multiplies by \(K(\nu)\). The ideal choice is a rect of width \(1/T\), which keeps the central copy and deletes the others; its inverse transform is the sinc, so ideal reconstruction is sinc interpolation:

$$ r(x)=\sum_n f(nT)\,k\!\left(\frac{x-nT}{T}\right), \qquad k_{\text{ideal}}(u)=\operatorname{sinc}(u)=\frac{\sin \pi u}{\pi u} $$

The box kernel gives \(K(\nu)=T\operatorname{sinc}(\nu T)\) instead — it leaks the neighbouring copies through as blockiness and sags to 0.64 of its value at the Nyquist frequency, about 3.9 dB of droop. Every kernel here equals 1 at the origin and 0 at the other sample points, which is what makes the curve pass through the samples.

Signal is a sum of cosines, so its spectrum is exact rather than estimated. Copies are drawn at equal height; the true amplitudes all carry the same 1/T factor.
