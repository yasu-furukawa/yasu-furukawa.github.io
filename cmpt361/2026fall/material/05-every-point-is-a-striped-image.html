Every point is a striped image

The Fourier transform of a picture is another picture. Each point in it stands for one striped pattern — the point’s position fixes the direction and spacing of the stripes, its amplitude fixes their contrast, and its phase slides them sideways. Click anywhere in the spectrum to pull out the stripe pattern hiding there.

Why a point means stripes. A point sitting at frequency (fx, fy) stands for the pattern cos(2π(fxx + fyy)/N − φ). Its bands run perpendicular to the line from the centre out to the point. Further from the centre means finer stripes; the direction from the centre sets which way they lean. A point on the horizontal axis gives vertical bands, and a point on the diagonal gives diagonal ones.

Phase is a shift, not a shape. This is the part that is easy to state and hard to see. Changing φ does not rotate the stripes or change their width — it only slides them along their own direction of travel, which is the arrow drawn on the panel. Everything about what the pattern looks like is in the position and the amplitude; the phase only says where the bands sit.

Points come in pairs. The image is made of real numbers, so the spectrum is symmetric about the centre: whatever sits at (fx, fy) is mirrored at (−fx, −fy), marked here with a hollow ring. The two of them together make one real striped pattern, which is why a single cosine shows up as two dots rather than one.

Two caveats worth saying out loud. A single frequency out of sixteen thousand is faint, so the third panel boosts contrast to make it visible — the number on the slider is the true amplitude. And the transform treats the image as tiling the plane forever, so a photo whose left edge does not match its right edge produces a bright cross through the centre. That cross is the seam, not the content.

Where to start. The brick wall is the most convincing: its strongest point sits at a spacing of about ten pixels, and the stripes it produces line up with the mortar lines in the photo. Then compare grass with moon — the grass spreads most of its energy far from the centre, the moon keeps nearly all of it close in, and that difference is visible before you click anything.
