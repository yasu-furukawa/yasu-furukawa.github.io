Inside K

Projection already got us from a 3D point to a ray. The intrinsic matrix is everything that happens after that: how a camera turns a ray into a pixel address. It holds just five numbers.

1Where K starts

A point X = (X,Y,Z) in camera coordinates projects by dividing out the depth:

(X, Y, Z)  ⟶  (x, y) = (X/Z,  Y/Z)
The result lives on the plane Z = 1, one unit in front of the camera centre. These normalized coordinates are pure slopes — no millimetres, no pixels, nothing about the sensor. A ray and its normalized point are the same thing.

Everything a camera adds on top of that is: where is my grid of light-collecting cells, and how big are they? That is what K encodes.

(u, v, 1)⊤ ∼ fscx 0αfcy 001 (x, y, 1)⊤

Written out, with no homogeneous mystery left in it:

u = f·x  +  s·y  +  cx
v = αf·y  +  cy

Convention used throughout: camera x right, y down, z forward; image u right, v down, origin at the top-left corner of the top-left pixel. With this choice every entry of K is positive and nothing is upside down.

Why some textbooks write −f

In the physical pinhole the sensor sits behind the centre of projection, so the image forms inverted. Books that draw it that way, and that also point y upward in the camera frame while v points downward in the image, have to carry two sign flips — and they bundle them into a negative focal length. We instead place a virtual image plane in front of the centre at z = 1 and point y down, which is what essentially all calibration code does. Same camera, no minus signs.

Push the sliders around and watch what each one owns:

  • f sets the spacing of the lattice. Small f → wide cells → the same cone of rays is covered by few pixels → wide field of view.
  • α sets the shape of a cell: tall or wide instead of square.
  • s sets the slant: the columns stop being perpendicular to the rows.
  • cx, cy set the placement: which ray gets to be pixel (0, 0).

The scene never moves. That is the point — K says nothing about the world, only about the measuring grid the camera lays over it.

Three rulers, one picture. The same blue rectangle is now marked three ways, and K is the conversion between the outer two.

Slope (grey, on the axes) — dimensionless, X/Z. This is what the ray gives you.
Centimetres (blue, on the rectangle) — where the ray lands on the physical sensor. The sensor sits at distance fcm, so one unit of slope is fcm centimetres across it.
Pixels (the grid) — cm divided by the pixel pitch p, that is multiplied by 1/p pixel/cm.

So the entry f of K is those two steps multiplied together: f = fcm · (1/p), with units [px / slope] = [cm / slope] · [px / cm]. Drag the sensor-width slider: fcm and 1/p both change, the cm labels change, and nothing in the picture moves — their product is all the camera ever knows. (Section 3 runs the same arithmetic in millimetres.)
Two ways to raise f, and they look different. The quad covers slopes of width W/f, so the picture depends on both numbers, and the two buttons under the f slider pick which one you hold still.

Same view, more pixels — shrink the pixels and keep the lens. W, H, c and s all scale with f, the blue outline holds still, the field of view never changes, and the grid simply gets finer. This is f in its plainest reading: pixels per unit slope.

Same image size — keep a 640×480 sensor and fit a longer lens. Now the fixed pixel budget is spread over a narrower cone, the outline shrinks, and the recorded image zooms in.

Only α and s change the shape of the quad. f changes its size or the grid's fineness, never its shape.

3f — the exchange rate between slope and pixels

A ray arrives at the camera with slope Y/Z. The sensor sits at distance fmm, so the ray lands y = fmm · Y/Z millimetres off the axis. The sensor is then chopped into cells of pitch p, so that distance is y/p pixels. Compose the two and the millimetres cancel.

A and B have completely different optics and sensors — and produce pixel-for-pixel identical images.
The only thing recoverable is the ratio. A 6 mm lens on a 3 µm sensor and a 12 mm lens on a 6 µm sensor both give f = 2000 px. No image can tell them apart, which is why calibration reports f in pixels and never in millimetres. fmm alone is a lens spec, not a camera parameter.

Two readings of the same number, both worth having in your head:

  • f is pixels per unit slope. A ray at slope 0.25 lands f/4 pixels from the principal point. Doubling f doubles every pixel displacement — objects look twice as big.
  • f fixes the field of view, but only together with the image size: FOVx = 2·arctan(W/2f). A bare focal length in pixels tells you nothing about the angle until you say how wide the image is — which is what the two buttons in section 2 were separating. For a 1920-wide frame, f ≈ 1500 px is a normal lens (≈ 65°), f ≈ 700 px is a wide angle (≈ 108°).

Useful rule of thumb when you have no calibration at all: for an ordinary photo, f ≈ the image width in pixels. It is rarely off by more than a factor of two.

4α — when pixels are not square

Nothing forces the sensor cells to be as tall as they are wide. If the horizontal pitch is px and the vertical pitch is py, you get two focal lengths, fx = fmm/px and fy = fmm/py, and their ratio α = fy/fx = px/py is the aspect ratio of one pixel.

A circle in the world comes back as an ellipse, and the ellipse is a property of the camera, not of the scene. Where it shows up in practice:

  • Digital video formats that sample a 4:3 frame into 720×480 or 720×576 — the stored pixels are deliberately non-square.
  • Anamorphic lenses, and any image you resized by different factors in x and y.
  • Some line-scan and industrial sensors.

On any modern CMOS sensor the cells are square to a fraction of a percent, so α = 1 is a safe assumption and most calibration code still estimates fx and fy separately — partly as a free sanity check, partly to soak up small modelling errors.

5s — skew, the entry that is almost always zero

Skew says the u and v axes of the pixel grid are not perpendicular. If the angle between them is θ, then s = −fx cot θ, so θ = 90° gives exactly s = 0. Drag the angle and watch the pixel turn into a parallelogram.

Why it exists at all, and why you can forget it:

  • It is a relic of digitised analogue video: a framegrabber sampling an analogue scanline with a clock not locked to the camera's own shears the sampling lattice. Nonzero skew was a real effect worth modelling in the 1990s.
  • On a solid-state sensor the cells are etched by lithography; the axes are perpendicular to within arcseconds. Measured skew on a real camera is indistinguishable from zero.
  • It can reappear artificially: if you shear or apply a general affine warp to an image, the K of the resulting "virtual camera" picks up a skew term.

So in practice K has four free numbers, not five. Fixing s = 0 is what makes self-calibration tractable — it is one of the constraints that buys you the Kruppa / absolute-conic machinery later in the course.

6cx, cy — the principal point

The principal point is where the optical axis pierces the image: the pixel hit by the ray with slope (0, 0). Two separate reasons it is not (0, 0) in pixel coordinates.

1. Bookkeeping

Pixel indices start at a corner, but the optical axis goes through the middle. Even for a perfectly built camera that already puts c ≈ (W/2, H/2). Most of what c is doing is just moving the origin from the centre to the corner.

2. Real misalignment

The sensor die is never mounted perfectly concentric with the lens axis — typically off by tens of pixels. And any crop or non-centred resize moves it further. The residual is what calibration actually estimates.

The principal point is also the centre of radial symmetry: radial distortion grows with distance from c, not from the image centre. Getting c wrong therefore bends your distortion model into a mess.

What crops and resizes do to K

This is the part that bites in practice. Every time you touch the image, K has to follow.

Cropping shifts c only. Resizing scales f and c together. Forgetting the second one is the single most common intrinsics bug.

Three things to carry out of this. K is affine — it maps lines to lines and never changes depth, so it cannot explain anything about 3D shape. It is also entirely a property of the camera, which is why it is called intrinsic and why one calibration serves every photo that camera takes. And lens distortion is not in here: it is nonlinear, it acts on the normalized coordinates before K, and it needs its own parameters.
