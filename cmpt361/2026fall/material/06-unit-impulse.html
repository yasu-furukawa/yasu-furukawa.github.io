The unit impulse

An impulse is not “a very tall spike.” It is a spike whose area is one. Everything confusing about δ comes from reading its height instead of its area — so this page never lets you see one without the other.

1 · Squeeze any unit-area bump

Start with a function that already has area 1 and a width knob σ. Shrink σ. The bump must grow taller at exactly the rate it grows narrower, because the area it encloses is not allowed to change. In the limit the height runs off to infinity and the area is still, stubbornly, 1.

So the limit object is not a function you can evaluate. Asking “what is δ(0)?” has no useful answer. The only question δ answers is how much area sits here, and that answer is 1.

Notation. We draw δ as an arrow, and the number we write beside the arrow is its area, never its height. An arrow labelled 3 means 3δ: area 3, height still infinite.

2 · Using a bump: multiply, then read the area

Here is a function given the way data usually arrives — as a set of samples with a curve through them. Slide a unit-area bump along it and multiply the two pointwise. The product is the third panel, and it is the only thing the impulse ever really does.

Narrow the bump and watch the third panel carefully. Its shape barely changes. What changes is the number on its vertical axis, which climbs without any limit at all — 5, then 20, then 200. The three panels are drawn with axes of identical length, so that climbing number is the honest report of how tall the product has become.

Then look at the area. It does not climb. It settles almost immediately on f(a) and stays there while the peak runs off to infinity. Between the two numbers the product gives you, the height is the one that has no limit and no meaning; the area is the one that answers a question.

3 · A finite train, and its exact spectrum

Put N boxes of width w in a row, spaced T. This object is finite, ordinary, and completely non-controversial, so its transform can be written down exactly with no distributions anywhere:

ŜN(u) = sinc(wu) · sin(πNuT)⁄sin(πuT)

One factor per ingredient. The box contributes the sinc — a fixed envelope that depends on w and nothing else. The repetition contributes the second factor, which is what builds the peaks: tall and narrow near u = k/T, small everywhere else. It is a perfectly ordinary continuous curve at every finite N.

One slider takes the whole limit. Pull it and two things happen together: more boxes, each one thinner. The boxes climb toward impulses in x, and in u the peaks do exactly what the bumps in Fig. 1 did — height rises as N, lobe width falls as 1/NT, and the area under each peak does not move. A quantity that concentrates without changing its area has only one place to go.

At the far end of the slider both halves of the picture are the same shape: a row of spikes spaced T below, a row of spikes spaced 1/T above. Nothing was ever declared to be an impulse — the curve simply got there.

4 · Sampling an image is this product

A sensor does not evaluate the world at a point. Each photosite is a little square window of width w, sitting on a grid of pitch Δ, and the number it reports is the light falling inside that window — which is the scene multiplied by the window and integrated. Lay the windows out across the row and you have a box train, one box per pixel. Sampling is that product, read off one box at a time.

pixel[k] = ∫ f(x) boxw(x − kΔ) dx

Two knobs, two familiar limits. The pitch Δ decides how many pixels you get. The aperture w decides what each one means: at full width each pixel is an honest average over its cell, and as w shrinks toward zero the box becomes an impulse and the pixel becomes a bare point sample of f. It is the product of Fig. 2 again, with the bump fixed to a grid and one box per pixel — and as there, what the pixel reads is the area, never the height.

The signal is a chirp — slow on the left, faster and faster to the right — because it makes the difference visible. Drop the aperture to a point and the fast end of the row comes back as a lazy, entirely fictional wave. Open the aperture and that region averages toward flat grey, which is the honest answer for detail the grid cannot hold.

What to carry out of here

  1. An impulse is an area, drawn as an arrow. Its height is not a number you are allowed to use.
  2. Any unit-area bump works. Gaussian, box, triangle, sinc — the limit is the same object, so choose whichever is convenient for the algebra in front of you.
  3. Repetition spaced T gives peaks spaced 1/T, and more repeats make them sharper without making them bigger. Peak height rises, width falls, area holds — the same trade-off as the bump, now in frequency. Push it far enough and you have a train of impulses on both sides.
  4. Multiplying by δ(x−a) and integrating samples f at a. Scale the impulse by c and you get c f(a): the scale factor is an area, so it comes straight through.
  5. Sampling is this product. A pixel is the scene times its aperture box, integrated — an area average that only becomes a point sample as the aperture shrinks. The width of that window is what stands between you and aliasing.
