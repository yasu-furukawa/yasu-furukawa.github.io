Images and Color

Ten slides from an image as the function I(x, c) through to CIELAB. Along the way: the MATLAB uint8 to im2double conversion, histograms, the cone and rod response curves, Bayer demosaicing, alpha compositing, and perceptual uniformity.

Try this

  • On slide 5, compare the three cone peaks. S sits at 420 nm, M at 534 nm, L at 564 nm. Notice how close M and L are, and that rods peak at 498 nm between them while carrying no hue at all.
  • On slide 6, count the green cells in the Bayer tile. Green appears twice as often as red or blue, which is why demosaicing has to interpolate two missing channels at every pixel.
  • On slide 8, read I = αF + (1−α)B against the three thumbnails, then think about a soft hair edge. Both F and B are unknown there, so the system is underdetermined.
  • Press A, mark up the histogram on slide 4, arrow away and come back. The strokes are stored per slide. C clears only the slide you are on.

Where the 360 degrees of hue actually come from.

Figure

The Hue Hexagon

H = atan2( √3·(G − B)/2 , R − (G + B)/2 )

A tilt slider rotates the camera around the RGB cube, from side-on with the gray axis vertical to straight down that axis. RGB sliders drop a point onto the midplane and draw its radius and hue arc, with live readouts for V, C, S, hexagonal H, and the true atan2 angle.

Try this

  • Drag tilt all the way from side-on to top-down. The six chromatic corners are not coplanar. They alternate above and below the midplane and form a crown. Only the view straight down the gray axis flattens them into a regular hexagon.
  • Set R = G = B and watch H. Hue is undefined on the gray axis, where chroma is zero.
  • Park the point exactly on a corner, then slide it partway toward the next one. Hexagonal H and the true angle agree at all six corners and drift apart by up to about 1.2 degrees in between.

Filtering, in three steps

These three build on each other. Impulses in 1D show which algebraic properties survive, continuous 1D shows the sliding integral producing a whole function, and 2D discrete pays it off in the frequency domain. Work through them in order.

Step 1
1D discrete

Convolution vs Correlation

(f * g)[n] = Σ f[k]·g[n − k] (f ⋆ g)[n] = Σ f[k]·g[n + k]

The two sums sit side by side with the one differing sign colored. Three sliders place unit impulses at a, b, and c. Two panels show where correlating them lands and flag live whether commutativity and associativity hold, checked against convolution doing the same thing.

Try this

  • Set a and b apart, then swap their values. Correlation gives δa ⋆ δb = δb−a, so swapping reflects the result. Convolution gives δa+b either way.
  • Slide a until it equals b. The commutativity flag for correlation flips to true, and that is the only case where it does, because both results land at 0.
  • Set a to something nonzero and read the associativity panel. The two groupings differ by exactly 2a. Set a to 0 and they collapse onto each other.

Step 2
1D continuous

Slide, Multiply, Integrate

(f * g)(t) = ∫ f(τ)·g(t − τ) dτ

Two panels hold f(τ) and g(τ), each a rect, triangle, ramp, or gaussian set by a center and a width. Dragging t slides g past f, shades the product under both curves, and reads out the integral. A panel underneath plots the whole result curve, solid up to the current t and faint beyond it.

Try this

  • Press Play and watch the lower panel fill in. Every t you sweep past adds one more point. The result of convolving two functions is itself a function, not a single number.
  • Flip the convolution and correlation toggle with the default shapes. The only thing that changes is g(t − τ) becoming g(t + τ). That flip is the entire mechanical difference between the two integrals.
  • Now make g a centered gaussian and flip again. The difference vanishes, which is why the default g is an asymmetric ramp.

Step 3
2D discrete

The Convolution Theorem

f ⊛ g ⟷ F ⊙ G

A commutative diagram with the spatial route across the top and the frequency route across the bottom, closed by three arrows for FFT, FFT, and inverse FFT. Both routes are computed live in the page at 128 × 128 with a hand-written radix-2 FFT. Five procedural images plus upload, seven kernels from impulse to Laplacian of Gaussian, and a size slider from 3 to 21.

Try this

  • Read the residual card. Two independent computations, and the maximum absolute difference lands near 1e-15. That number is the proof of the theorem, not an assertion about it.
  • Cycle the kernels and watch |G| by itself. Box and gaussian are bright in the middle and fade outward, so they are low-pass. LoG is a ring that goes dark at DC, so it is band-pass. The impulse δ gives 1 everywhere.
  • Hover any spectrum panel to read (u, v) in cycles per image. DC sits at the center because the spectra are fftshifted, so distance from the middle is frequency.
  • Push the kernel size up and look at the border of the result. The direct sum is deliberately circular so that it matches the FFT exactly. The note explains the zero-padding to N + K − 1 you need for the honest non-circular answer.
